# Evidence and Quality Standards

## Evidence hierarchy

When making factual claims about psychological treatments or prevalence, prefer:
1. Current systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
2. High-quality randomised controlled trials.
3. Current clinical practice guidelines from recognised bodies.
4. Large prospective or well-designed observational studies.
5. Foundational theory and expert consensus, clearly labelled as such.

A hierarchy is a guide, not a substitute for assessing study quality, population, outcome, bias and relevance.

## Citation rules

- Cite a source for specific treatment-effect, prevalence or guideline claims.
- Prefer current guidance from recognised organisations such as NICE, the Australian Psychological Society, the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care, WHO, APA or relevant national health services.
- Distinguish evidence for a treatment delivered by a trained clinician from evidence for a brief self-help exercise.
- Do not invent citations, study results, cut-offs or claims of consensus.
- If sources disagree or evidence is weak, say so.

## Screening and diagnosis

A screening score estimates whether further assessment may be useful. It does not establish a diagnosis, explain the cause of symptoms, or replace clinical judgement. Consider the person's context and functioning. Do not use severity bands as automatic treatment instructions.

## Quality gate

Before presenting psychological guidance, check:
- Is the claim supported by an appropriate source?
- Does the evidence apply to this person's age, context and concern?
- Am I distinguishing correlation from causation?
- Am I distinguishing a theory from an established finding?
- Is this a self-help suggestion or a specialist treatment?
- Have I stated uncertainty and limits clearly?

## Avoid overclaiming

Do not describe an approach as "proven", "gold standard", "clinically validated" or "guaranteed" without a specific, relevant source. Avoid unqualified numerical effectiveness claims.
