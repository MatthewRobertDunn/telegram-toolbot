# Exercise Library

This is an original, concise library of ACT-informed practices. Exercises are optional and should be adapted to the person and context. Ask permission before guiding a substantial practice. The person can stop or change any exercise.

## 1. Notice and name the process

**Targets:** present-moment awareness, defusion, functional awareness.

**Use when:** the person feels swept up in an experience and wants help slowing down.

**Guide:**
1. Pause and identify what is happening externally.
2. Name any thoughts, feelings, sensations, and urges.
3. Use neutral labels: "worry is here", "there is tension", "the urge to avoid is present."
4. Ask what response would be useful now.

**Reflect:** "What became clearer when you named the different parts?"

**Caution:** Do not use as a substitute for immediate safety or practical action.

## 2. I'm having the thought that...

**Targets:** cognitive defusion.

**Use when:** a thought feels like an unquestionable fact or command.

**Guide:**
1. Choose a manageable thought.
2. Say it once as usual.
3. Repeat it with "I'm having the thought that..."
4. Notice whether the relationship to it shifts, even slightly.
5. Ask whether the thought needs practical attention.

**Reflect:** "Did anything change in how tightly the thought held your attention?"

**Caution:** Do not imply the thought is false or unimportant.

## 3. Name the story

**Targets:** cognitive defusion, self-as-context.

**Use when:** a familiar self-critical or threat-focused narrative repeats.

**Guide:** Work with the person to give the recurring story a neutral, perhaps gently humorous name. When it appears, they can say, "There's the [name] story again." Then return to the situation and choose a response.

**Reflect:** "What does this story urge you to do? Is that what you want to follow right now?"

**Caution:** Avoid labels that shame or trivialise the person's experience.

## 4. Leaves on a stream

**Targets:** defusion, present-moment awareness.

**Use when:** the person likes imagery and wants to practise observing thoughts.

**Guide:** Invite them to imagine a stream with leaves moving along it. As thoughts arise, they can place each on a leaf and watch it travel. If no image appears, simply label thoughts and let attention return to the present.

**Reflect:** "What was it like to notice thoughts without needing to follow each one?"

**Caution:** The aim is not to force thoughts away. Stop if imagery is uncomfortable.

## 5. Gentle expansion

**Targets:** acceptance, willingness, present-moment awareness.

**Use when:** the person is struggling with an uncomfortable but manageable internal sensation and wants to practise making room.

**Guide:** Invite them to notice a mild sensation, locate it if comfortable, describe it, and allow it some space without forcing it to change. Keep attention partly connected to the room. Remind them they can stop.

**Reflect:** "Was there a way to let the sensation be present without giving it the whole stage?"

**Caution:** Avoid intense sensations, traumatic memories, or practices that increase panic or dissociation. Do not use for medical symptoms that need assessment.

## 6. Thank your mind

**Targets:** defusion.

**Use when:** the mind repeatedly offers warnings or criticism.

**Guide:** When a familiar thought appears, try a gentle "Thanks, mind" and notice what action is available. The phrase is an acknowledgement, not a command to dismiss the thought.

**Reflect:** "Could you hear the warning without automatically following it?"

## 7. Five-senses orientation

**Targets:** present-moment contact.

**Use when:** the person wants a simple way to reconnect with immediate surroundings.

**Guide:** Invite them to notice a few things they can see, hear, and physically feel. Keep it brief and let them choose what to notice. Eyes can remain open.

**Reflect:** "What do you notice about the room and this moment now?"

**Caution:** This is not a guaranteed grounding technique. Stop or adapt if it feels uncomfortable.

## 8. The choice point

**Targets:** functional analysis, values, committed action.

**Use when:** the person sees a recurring pattern between discomfort and behaviour.

**Guide:**
1. Identify a specific situation.
2. Name the difficult thoughts and feelings.
3. Identify the urge.
4. Explore an away move: what action reduces discomfort or moves away from a chosen value?
5. Explore a towards move: what action would express a value?
6. Choose one realistic response.

**Reflect:** "Which direction would you like your next action to move?"

**Caution:** Away/towards is not a moral judgement. Leaving danger or setting a boundary can be a towards move.

## 9. Values compass

**Targets:** values clarification.

**Use when:** the person feels directionless or is deciding what matters in a situation.

**Guide:**
1. Choose one life area.
2. Ask what qualities of action matter there.
3. Separate the person's own values from expectations or pressure.
4. Identify one small way to express a chosen quality today.

**Reflect:** "What would this value look like in an ordinary, observable action?"

**Caution:** Do not push for a grand purpose or assume all life areas matter equally.

## 10. One small committed step

**Targets:** committed action.

**Use when:** a person knows what matters but feels stuck or overwhelmed.

**Guide:**
1. Name the value.
2. Identify the smallest meaningful action.
3. Check feasibility and safety.
4. Name one likely barrier.
5. Make a flexible if-then plan.
6. Ask whether the person genuinely chooses it.

**Reflect:** "Does this step feel small enough to begin, and meaningful enough to matter?"

## 11. Observer perspective: noticing change

**Targets:** self-as-context.

**Use when:** a person is fused with a global self-description and finds perspective-taking helpful.

**Guide:** Invite them to notice a sound, a sensation, and a thought. Ask whether each is something they can notice. Explore the difference between having an experience and being defined by it.

**Reflect:** "What does this perspective let you include that the label leaves out?"

**Caution:** Avoid with people who experience dissociation, depersonalisation, or destabilisation from observer exercises. Return to concrete external orientation.

## 12. Cost of the struggle

**Targets:** functional analysis, acceptance, values.

**Use when:** repeated attempts to control an internal experience are taking substantial effort.

**Guide:**
1. Identify what the person has tried to control or avoid.
2. Acknowledge how the strategy may help in the short term.
3. Explore costs, if any, over time.
4. Ask what they might do differently if the struggle did not have to come first.
5. Consider whether acceptance, practical change, support, or a boundary is most useful.

**Reflect:** "What would you like to spend more of your energy on?"

**Caution:** Do not imply that all coping strategies are bad or that the person should stop useful treatment or protection.

## Exercise selection rule

Choose the simplest relevant practice. If the person is distressed, first listen and assess immediate needs. Do not stack exercises. One exercise followed by reflection is usually enough.
