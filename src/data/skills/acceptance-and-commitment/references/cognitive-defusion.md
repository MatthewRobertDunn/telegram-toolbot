# Cognitive Defusion

## Purpose

Cognitive defusion means changing how a person relates to thoughts. Thoughts can be noticed as words, images, memories, predictions, rules, or stories produced by the mind. They may contain useful information, but they do not automatically have to dictate behaviour.

Defusion is not thought suppression, distraction, positive thinking, or proving a thought wrong.

## When it may help

Consider defusion when a person:
- treats a thought as an unquestionable fact;
- is caught in repetitive mental argument;
- follows a thought as though it were a command;
- waits for a thought to disappear before acting;
- is struggling with a harsh self-story.

Do not use defusion to dismiss a realistic concern that needs practical attention. A thought may point to a genuine problem that deserves action.

## Common practices

### "I'm having the thought that..."
Invite the person to notice the thought and add the phrase "I'm having the thought that..." This can create a little distance without denying the thought's content.

### Name the story
Give a recurring mental pattern a neutral name, such as "the not-good-enough story" or "the disaster forecast." The label should feel useful to the person, not mocking.

### Repeat a word
If appropriate and comfortable, repeat a single word from a thought slowly for a short period and notice its sound and quality. Do not use this if it feels silly, invalidating, or unhelpful.

### Thank the mind
Try "Thanks, mind" when the mind produces a familiar warning. This is not sarcasm or dismissal; it acknowledges the mind's attempt to protect.

### Leaves on a stream
Invite the person to imagine placing each thought on a leaf floating along a stream and watching it move away. If visual imagery is difficult, use a simpler metaphor such as passing cars or notifications. This is an optional exercise, not a requirement to make thoughts disappear.

## A brief guided practice

1. Ask permission.
2. Invite the person to choose a mildly sticky thought, not the most distressing memory they have.
3. Ask them to notice the exact words.
4. Try "I'm noticing the thought that..."
5. Ask what they notice in their experience.
6. Ask whether the thought still needs practical attention.
7. If relevant, identify one action that serves a chosen value whether or not the thought remains.

## Common problems

- **"The thought is true."** Defusion does not require deciding it is false. Ask whether treating it as a command is useful and what evidence or practical action deserves attention.
- **"The thought didn't go away."** That is not failure. The aim is more choice, not disappearance.
- **"This feels fake."** Stop or adapt. Not every technique suits every person.
- **The thought signals real danger.** Prioritise safety and practical action rather than defusing away a warning.

## Language to use

Prefer: "Can you notice that your mind is offering this thought?"

Avoid: "That's just a thought, so ignore it."
