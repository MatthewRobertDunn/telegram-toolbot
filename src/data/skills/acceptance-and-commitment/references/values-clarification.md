# Values Clarification

## What values are

Values are chosen qualities of action and ongoing directions for living. They describe how a person wants to show up in areas of life, even when outcomes are uncertain.

Examples might include being caring in relationships, curious in learning, fair at work, playful with friends, or courageous when facing uncertainty. These are examples only; never assign them to the person.

## Values versus goals

- **Value:** an ongoing direction, such as being a supportive friend.
- **Goal:** an outcome, such as calling a friend this week.
- **Action:** a specific behaviour, such as sending a message today.

Goals can be completed. Values are enacted repeatedly and can guide many different goals.

## Clarifying questions

- "What kind of person would you like to be in this situation?"
- "What matters to you about this area of life?"
- "If nobody were judging or rewarding you, what would still feel meaningful?"
- "What would you like your actions to stand for?"
- "Which part of this is genuinely yours, and which part feels imposed?"
- "How would this value show up in a small ordinary moment?"

## Life domains

Explore only domains relevant to the person. Possible areas include:
- relationships and family;
- friendship and community;
- work, study, and contribution;
- health and caring for the body;
- learning and personal growth;
- recreation, creativity, and play;
- spirituality or connection;
- citizenship, fairness, or the natural world.

Do not imply that every domain must be important or balanced equally.

## Values Bullseye-inspired reflection

Invite the person to consider one chosen life area:
1. What matters to me here?
2. How have my recent actions lined up with that direction?
3. What has made it harder?
4. What is one small action that would move me closer?

Avoid scoring the person as good or bad. A reflection is information, not a verdict.

## Values conflicts

People can hold multiple important values that pull in different directions. Explore the trade-off with curiosity. Avoid declaring one value superior. Look for a workable action that honours the most important considerations, including safety, responsibilities, and limits.

## When values work becomes unhelpful

Pause if it turns into:
- a search for the one perfect life purpose;
- pressure to be productive;
- shame about past choices;
- a list of obligations inherited from others;
- an attempt to guarantee an outcome.

Values should orient action, not become another weapon for self-criticism.
