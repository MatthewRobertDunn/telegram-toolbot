# ACT Foundations

## What ACT is

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) is a contextual behavioural approach that aims to increase psychological flexibility. It focuses on changing how a person responds to thoughts, feelings, memories, bodily sensations, and urges, while helping them act in ways that serve chosen values.

ACT does not assume that distress is always caused by faulty thinking. Human language and learning can make painful thoughts feel literal, urgent, and controlling. Attempts to avoid or control private experiences can sometimes narrow life further.

ACT is not passive acceptance. It combines openness to internal experience with active, values-guided engagement in the world.

## The six processes

### Acceptance
Willingly making room for feelings, sensations, memories, or urges when struggling with them is unworkable. Acceptance is active and chosen, not forced. It can coexist with problem-solving and changing external conditions.

### Cognitive defusion
Noticing thoughts as mental events. The aim is not to prove thoughts false or make them vanish, but to loosen their automatic control over behaviour.

### Present-moment contact
Bringing flexible attention to current experience. This includes the outside world, the body, thoughts, emotions, and the task at hand. Mindfulness is not restricted to formal meditation.

### Self-as-context
The perspective from which experiences are noticed. A person can observe changing thoughts and feelings without being defined by any one of them. Use this gently; do not make metaphysical claims.

### Values
Chosen life directions and qualities of action. Values are ongoing and personally meaningful. They are not goals to complete or standards imposed by others.

### Committed action
Building patterns of behaviour that serve values. This includes setting goals, practising skills, making room for barriers, learning from setbacks, and adapting plans.

## How the processes fit together

The six processes are overlapping ways to describe psychological flexibility. They are not a rigid sequence or a checklist. A person may need practical action before values work, grounding before defusion, or support with external problem-solving before acceptance is relevant.

The hexaflex is often grouped into three broad pairs:
- Openness: acceptance and defusion.
- Awareness: present-moment contact and self-as-context.
- Engagement: values and committed action.

These groupings are teaching aids, not separate compartments.

## ACT versus common misunderstandings

| ACT is | ACT is not |
|---|---|
| Making room for experience while choosing behaviour | Suppressing feelings |
| Noticing thoughts as thoughts | Replacing every negative thought with a positive one |
| Acting in line with chosen values | Chasing goals to earn worth |
| Flexible attention | Forcing calm or emptying the mind |
| Willingness when useful | Enduring danger or abuse |
| Contextual, functional understanding | Labelling a person as broken or resistant |

## Functional contextualism in plain language

Ask what behaviour does in a particular context, not merely what it looks like. The same action can serve different functions for different people or at different times. Avoidance may protect someone from genuine danger in one context and restrict a valued life in another.

## Companion guidance

Use everyday language first. Introduce technical terms only when they clarify something. Do not teach a lecture when a short reflection would be more useful.
