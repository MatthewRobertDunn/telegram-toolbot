# Functional Analysis

## Purpose

Functional analysis explores how behaviour works in context. It helps identify what tends to happen before and after a behaviour, what the behaviour accomplishes in the short term, and how it affects the person's life over time.

It is not an interrogation, diagnosis, or search for blame.

## A simple map

1. **Context:** Where were you, what was happening, and what was at stake?
2. **Trigger:** What event, thought, memory, sensation, or interaction set the pattern in motion?
3. **Private experience:** What thoughts, emotions, body sensations, images, and urges appeared?
4. **Behaviour:** What did you do, avoid, say, check, postpone, or seek?
5. **Immediate consequence:** What changed right away? Relief, certainty, connection, escape, protection, or something else?
6. **Longer-term consequence:** What happened later? Did the response support or restrict what matters?
7. **Alternative response:** What other action might be available, given the actual context and risks?

## Short-term relief and long-term cost

Avoidance is often understandable because it works in the short term. Cancelling a difficult conversation may reduce anxiety immediately. Over time, it may leave an important issue unresolved.

Do not assume avoidance is always harmful. Withdrawing from danger, taking rest, or declining an unreasonable demand may be protective and values-consistent. Examine context and function.

## Conversational method

Ask one question at a time. Start with a recent, concrete example rather than asking the person to explain their whole life.

Useful prompts:
- "Can we slow down and look at one recent time this happened?"
- "What was going on just before you noticed the urge?"
- "What did doing that help with in the moment?"
- "What happened afterwards?"
- "What mattered to you in that situation?"
- "What options would respect both your needs and your safety?"

## Build hypotheses, not verdicts

Say "I wonder if postponing gives you a little relief from uncertainty" rather than "You procrastinate because you fear failure."

Check whether the interpretation fits. Be ready to revise it.

## Example

Situation: A person wants to send an application.

- Trigger: Opening the application form.
- Private experience: "I'll get it wrong"; tension; urge to close the page.
- Behaviour: Switches to unrelated tasks.
- Immediate consequence: Anxiety drops.
- Longer-term consequence: Deadline approaches; opportunity may be lost; self-criticism grows.
- Possible alternative: Open the form for five minutes, allow uncertainty to be present, and complete one section.

The alternative is only useful if applying serves a value the person actually endorses. The companion should not assume that every opportunity should be pursued.
